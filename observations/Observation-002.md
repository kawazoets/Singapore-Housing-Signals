# Observation 002

## Singapore Housing Policy Appears to Respond to Momentum, Not Just Price Levels

**Date: 2026-08-19**

While building Singapore Housing Signals, I initially focused on long-term movements in the HDB Resale Price Index and the Private Residential Property Price Index.

However, comparing these price series with major housing-policy interventions revealed a more interesting pattern.

The Singapore Government does not appear to respond simply to whether housing prices are high or low.

What seems to matter more is **how quickly prices are changing, how persistent that movement is, and which segment of the housing market is accelerating.**

## From Price Level to Price Momentum

This distinction matters.

A conventional housing-market analysis often asks:

> How high are property prices now?

Singapore Housing Signals asks a different question:

> How quickly are prices moving, and what does the Government do when that momentum changes?

Looking at the quarters preceding major policy interventions shows several different patterns.

| Policy Event | HDB Price Change – Previous 2 Quarters | Private Price Change – Previous 2 Quarters |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 2010 SSD introduction | +7.5% | +24.2% |
| 2010 SSD tightening | +6.9% | +11.2% |
| 2011 SSD tightening | +6.7% | +5.7% |
| 2013 ABSD tightening | +4.5% | +2.4% |
| 2018 ABSD tightening | -0.7% | +7.4% |
| 2021 Cooling Measures | +6.0% | +1.9% |
| 2022 HDB measures | +5.3% | +4.2% |
| 2023 ABSD tightening | +3.3% | +3.7% |
| 2024 HDB LTV tightening | +4.1% | +2.3% |
| 2026 removal of 15-month wait-out period | -0.4% | +1.4% |

The numbers do not suggest a simple mechanical threshold.

There is no obvious rule such as:

> “If quarterly price growth exceeds X%, the Government introduces cooling measures.”

Instead, the pattern appears to be **segment-specific and context-dependent**.

## 2018 Is an Important Example

In 2018, HDB resale prices were relatively stable.

Private residential prices, however, had accelerated significantly.

The Government responded with measures directed primarily at the private residential market, including higher Additional Buyer’s Stamp Duty (ABSD) rates and tighter Loan-to-Value limits.

This suggests that the Government does not necessarily treat the housing market as one single market.

It can respond to overheating in a particular segment.

## 2021 Shows a Different Pattern

By late 2021, both the private residential and HDB resale markets were showing sustained price increases.

When the Government announced cooling measures in December 2021, it explicitly referred to **“clear upward momentum”** in both markets.

This wording is significant.

It suggests that the **direction and persistence of price movement** are themselves part of the policy signal.

## 2026 Shows the Reverse Direction

The same framework can also be observed when policy is eased.

By 2026, HDB resale price momentum had weakened substantially.

The Government subsequently removed the 15-month wait-out period that had applied to private residential property owners purchasing non-subsidised HDB resale flats.

The policy explanation referred to several quarters of price moderation and signs of market stabilisation.

This is the reverse of the earlier cooling pattern:

**Acceleration → Tightening**

but also:

**Moderation / Stabilisation → Normalisation**

## A Preliminary Policy Reaction Function

Based on these observations, the first working model of Singapore Housing Signals is:

**Price Momentum × Persistence × Market Segment**

↓

**Government Policy Response**

This is not intended to be a predictive formula.

Housing policy also depends on affordability, interest rates, transaction activity, housing supply, financial stability and broader economic conditions.

The purpose of the framework is narrower:

> To observe what the Government appears to be responding to before focusing on what property prices may do next.

## Why This Matters

Most property-market reports describe what has already happened.

Prices increased.

Transactions declined.

Supply increased.

A policy was introduced.

Singapore Housing Signals attempts to connect these events differently.

Instead of asking only:

> What happened to the housing market?

the project asks:

> **What change in the housing market appears to have caused the Government to act?**

That distinction is the starting point for observing Singapore’s housing-policy reaction function.
